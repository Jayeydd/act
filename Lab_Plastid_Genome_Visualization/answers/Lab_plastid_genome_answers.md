# Lab Plastid Genome Answers — Ginkgo biloba (MN443423.1)

1. **What is the scientific name of your chosen plant and its plastid genome accession number?**
   * The plant is *Ginkgo biloba* L. (maidenhair tree). Its plastid genome accession number is MN443423.1.

2. **What is the total length of the plastid genome in base pairs?**
   * The plastid genome is 156,990 bp long.
     
3. **Can you identify the LSC, SSC, IRa, and IRb regions on your map? Briefly describe their positions.**
   * **LSC (Large Single-Copy):** 99,259 bp. It is the biggest region, covering the top of the circle and reaching down both sides.
   * **SSC (Small Single-Copy):** 22,267 bp. It is at the bottom of the circle.
   * **IRa (Inverted Repeats):** 17,732 bp. It is at the lower right, between the SSC and the start of the LSC.
   * **IRb (Inverted Repeats):** 17,732 bp. It is at the lower left of the circle, between the end of the LSC and the SSC.
   
    The two IRs are inverted copies of the same sequence, and the LSC and SSC sit between them. The sizes add up to the genome length: 99,259 + 17,732 + 22,267 + 17,732 = 156,990 bp. I took the sizes from Yang et al. (2021), because the GenBank record does not label the repeats. The black tick marks on the inner ring show where the regions meet.
 
4. **Give three examples of genes located in the LSC region.**
   * *psbA*, *rbcL*, and *atpB* are in the LSC

5. **Give at least one example of a gene located in the SSC region.**
   * *ndhF* is in the SSC. (also *ycf1*, *rps15*)

6. **Give at least one example of a gene that occurs within an inverted repeat region. Is the gene shown more than once because of the duplicated IR regions?**
   * *rrn16* is in the inverted repeat. Yes, it is shown twice on the map, once in IRa and once in IRb, because the two IRs are copies of the same sequence. *rps7*, *rps12* and *ndhB* are also shown twice.

7. **Choose one photosynthesis-related gene visible on your map. State its gene name and its general biological function.**
   * The gene is *psbA*. It codes for the D1 protein of the Photosystem II reaction center. D1 binds the cofactors that carry out the first charge separation in photosynthesis, and it takes part in splitting water.
     
8. **What does the direction of the gene arrows or gene orientation tell you about transcription on the plastid genome?**
   * The map has two grey arrows near the top. The one outside the ring points counterclockwise, and the one inside the ring points clockwise. This means genes drawn on the outside of the ring are transcribed counterclockwise and genes drawn on the inside are transcribed clockwise. So genes are transcribed from both strands of the plastid DNA, not in a single direction, and neighboring genes can be read in opposite directions.

9. **Look at the GC content graph. Does GC content appear uniform across the entire plastid genome, or does it vary among regions? Describe what you observe without over-interpreting it.**
   * The GC content is not uniform. The graph goes up and down along the genome. The band looks thicker around IRa and IRb than across most of the LSC, so the inverted repeats appear to have higher GC content. The small single-copy SSC regions display lower overall GC content and also show more fluctuation, with the profile rising and falling more noticeably throughout these areas. There is no flat, consistent line across the whole genome. Although the overall GC content is 39.56%, so most of the genome is still AT-rich.

10. **Why is a graphical plastid genome map useful compared with viewing only the raw DNA sequence or a long list of annotations?**
    * A map lets you see the whole genome at once. You can see where the LSC, SSC and IRs are, which genes are in each region, which genes are repeated in the IR, and which direction genes are transcribed. The GC graph also shows variation along the genome. With a raw DNA sequence or a long list of genes, you would have to read through thousands of lines and work out the positions yourself.
